# Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a common chronic liver disorder that spans steatosis, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), fibrosis and cirrhosis [ref: MASLD and MASH nomenclature consensus]. Fibrosis stage is strongly associated with liver-related and overall outcomes [ref: fibrosis stage and clinical outcomes in MASLD cohorts]. Yet how inherited genetic variation relates to the multicellular remodeling of established human disease is still incompletely mapped. Liver samples with similar histological diagnoses can contain different mixtures of hepatocyte stress, immune activation, stromal remodeling and vascular dysfunction, so tissue and cell context are needed to interpret them. Histology is therefore an essential clinical anchor, but it does not fully describe molecular state. A data-driven molecular continuum, following the framework of Kamzolas et al. (2026; doi:10.1038/s42255-026-01543-7), can add resolution within recorded stages without replacing pathological assessment.

Human genetics and disease-tissue transcriptomics approach this biology from different directions. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and fine-mapping identify inherited trait-associated variation. This variation can include protein-altering variants, noncoding variants with regulatory evidence and signals whose molecular mechanism is unresolved [ref: statistical fine-mapping methods]. Colocalization with molecular quantitative trait loci (QTL) can connect some trait associations to gene regulation [ref: colocalization methods, including multi-signal colocalization]. However, colocalization alone does not establish which gene mediates a trait association. It also does not require the gene to stay differentially expressed once disease is established. Some protein-altering variants may act through protein function without changing steady-state RNA, while others change RNA abundance through nonsense-mediated decay or splicing (Rivas et al., 2015). Conversely, differential expression captures active changes in tissue state but cannot tell inherited drivers apart from adaptive responses, shifts in cell composition or consequences of chronic injury. The two maps therefore answer different but connected biological questions.

Recent studies have produced large liver expression QTL (eQTL) resources [ref: human liver eQTL resources], multicohort transcriptomic analyses [ref: multicohort MASLD liver transcriptome analyses], single-cell and spatial MASLD atlases [ref: single-cell and spatial human MASLD liver atlases] and multilayer gene-prioritization frameworks [ref: multilayer MASLD gene-prioritization frameworks]. These resources have nominated risk loci, disease-associated cell states, fibrosis programs and potential therapeutic mechanisms. Kendall et al. (2023) linked genes to clinical outcomes in a multimodal MASLD database, and LiRNA (Alvarez-Sola et al., 2026) made harmonized human liver RNA-seq interactively queryable. Neither carries a fine-mapped inherited-variation layer next to its expression layer, so neither can say whether the two point to the same genes. Other studies have linked parts of the two maps. A random-effects meta-analysis of 10 biopsy datasets (1,058 samples) integrated MASLD and MASH differential expression with GWAS and coexpression modules (Piras and DiStefano, 2024). A GWAS meta-analysis paired with liver RNA-seq and genotypes from 504 participants nominated the long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) TRIB1AL by colocalization (Gobeil et al., 2026a). Surrogate-trait GWAS, colocalization and CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) in hepatocytes identified VKORC1, TNKS, LYPLAL1 and GPAM as regulators of lipid accumulation (Saliba-Gustafsson et al., 2025). Partitioned polygenic scores separated liver-confined from systemic MASLD (Jamialahmadi et al., 2024).

Multi-ancestry GWAS have been combined with genetically predicted gene expression to prioritize MASLD genes (Seagle et al., 2025). Single-cell eQTL mapping in liver biopsies from 25 people with MASLD and 23 controls found cell-state-dependent eQTLs enriched for MASLD heritability (Hong et al., 2025). Liver chromatin accessibility, reporter-assay, liver eQTL and CRISPR interference data have been combined to assign target genes to MASLD risk variants (Zhu et al., 2026). None of these studies keeps colocalization of fine-mapped multi-ancestry GWAS with a European liver-eQTL panel, multicohort disease-state expression and donor-aware cell and tissue context side by side for each gene. Here, donor-aware means that the donor, not the cell, is the unit of inference. What is missing is a per-gene record of both kinds of evidence that also states, for each layer, whether the gene was untested or was tested without support. Such a record has to keep the biological meaning, coverage, source and unit of inference of each assay, rather than reduce them to a count of modalities.

Here, we assembled a multimodal human MASLD Resource that keeps these two kinds of evidence side by side (Figure 1). The transcriptomic arm includes bulk liver RNA sequencing from nine cohorts. Of these, five cohorts have controls, and their 774 genetic-identity clusters entered a pooled, cohort-adjusted disease-versus-control analysis (Figure 3). These clusters are not verified independent people. The genetic arm combines prespecified GWAS strata across ancestry groups with liver eQTL colocalization, fine-mapped coding and noncoding context, and a reanalysis of a published liver chromatin-accessibility QTL map (Currin et al., 2025). It keeps direct disease and imaging phenotypes separate from liver-enzyme proxy traits (Figure 2). Single-cell analyses score prespecified gene programs in each cell lineage, with donors as the unit of inference (Figure 4). Protein, chromatin-accessibility and spatial assays add molecular and physical context, each in its native unit (Figure 5). We retain fibrosis stage in the primary severity analyses. We also add an externally anchored transcriptomic continuum to describe molecular variation among participants assigned the same stage.

We follow two recurring examples through these layers. Both were selected from prespecified analyses, not from a count of supporting modalities. GNMT has a single-signal colocalization with NAFLD, a direct disease trait, but no multi-signal colocalization call, and its NAFLD association is sub-threshold (p = 2.6 × 10⁻⁴). Its Catalog genetics call is therefore indeterminate, and its local regulatory haplotype is unresolved. Together with MAT1A and CYP2C19, GNMT has lower disease-state RNA, lower abundance at higher fibrosis stage in cross-sectional comparisons, and lower liver protein. We do not claim that this three-gene metabolic state reflects one inherited mechanism. The second example is a pair of programs, ECM/IGFBP7 and ductular-injury/BICC1. We use them to test whether a program's disease association, its cell-of-origin assignment and its organization in intact tissue support the same conclusion. In bulk RNA from the 364 units, controls included, of the three cohorts that record both an exact fibrosis stage and NAS, the fibrosis association of ECM/IGFBP7 does not reach Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) q < 0.05. In single-cell data from 60 donors, neither program has a cross-sectional stage association that passes BH correction across all 117 programs, with p values from stage labels shuffled within each dataset (Supplementary Note 2). Ambient RNA is RNA from other cells that contaminates single-cell profiles. Program scores before and after ambient correction do not let us assign either program to hepatocytes. In spatial transcriptomics, of the two programs only ECM/IGFBP7 is more spatially clustered than matched control genes, but most of the 114 scorable fixed programs also pass this comparison, so the result does not show program-specific organization. Ductular-injury/BICC1 is indeterminate (Figure 5F).

We then compared the two maps gene by gene. Of the 14,704 genes that both analyses list, multi-signal colocalization evaluated 3,769. Among these 3,769, 426 were colocalized and 220 were disease-state calls. The two sets shared 26 genes, against 21.2 expected within expression deciles (Mantel-Haenszel odds ratio 1.29, 95% CI 0.84 to 2.01). Because expression was not fully controlled, we state no direction. The overlap is also not informative on its own. The interval includes 1, the unstratified test was underpowered across a wide range of odds ratios, and two sets of these sizes are mostly disjoint whether or not the underlying biology differs. Across all 14,704 shared genes, 935 of the 1,129 disease-state calls without colocalization were tested only by single-signal colocalization and had no multi-signal result, so they are not genetic negatives (Figure 1; Supplementary Note 3). The Gene Catalog gives such genes an indeterminate genetic call. Its untested class holds genes untestable in either domain, including 698 of the 699 disease-state calls that lie outside the 14,704 genes and have no tier-1/2 colocalization record (Figure 6D). Matched for expression, colocalized genes are not more hepatocyte-specific than disease-state genes, so the prespecified directional prediction failed. The difference we observed ran the other way, but that comparison is post hoc and does not establish confirmatory class separation (Supplementary Note 3). We detected no difference between the two classes in the liver protein response to fibrosis stage (73 pairs). This is not evidence of equivalence. Pooled across ancestries, fine-mapped credible sets at genome-wide-significant leads for direct MASLD and liver-fat phenotypes carry 29.65% of their posterior probability in protein-altering or canonical-splice variants. The value is 6.67% for the liver enzymes alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT). This contrast mixes trait class with GWAS ancestry and fine-mapping coverage, so it receives no trait-class call (Figure 2; Supplementary Note 4).

The resulting MASLD Gene Catalog gives each protein-coding gene, lncRNA transcript and unresolved locus separate evidence states for inherited variation and for disease-state RNA. It links genes to cell programs by membership, attaches protein, chromatin and spatial context at the program level, and proposes a next experiment (Figure 6). The record separates an inherited candidate for perturbation from a disease-state change that could serve as its readout. For 26,678 of the 28,233 genes (94.5%), the record names no specific assay, and the proposed experiment is to obtain the missing evidence layer. Finally, we score predictive models against each task's own baseline and report each score beside a per-study record of the model's training exposure (Figure 7). This per-gene record, which keeps the two kinds of evidence apart and separates untested genes from genes tested without support, is the standalone contribution of the Resource.
