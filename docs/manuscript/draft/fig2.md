## Multi-signal liver eQTL colocalization links 436 genes to 35 liver-trait GWAS strata

To map inherited variation relevant to MASLD, we assembled a prespecified set of direct-disease, MRI-derived liver-fat, and liver-enzyme GWAS from several ancestry groups (Figure 2A) [ref: source publications for the 35 GWAS strata]. We kept direct disease and imaging traits apart from the ALT, AST, and GGT proxy phenotypes. Enzyme associations report liver biology but are not equivalent to direct MASLD susceptibility. The registry contains 35 tier-1/2 strata from five GWAS ancestries and six liver traits. Fine-mapping retained 360 trait-specific loci, which correspond to 265 globally unique loci [ref: SuSiE fine-mapping].

Colocalization against the Broadaway liver-eQTL panel (Broadaway et al., 2024) gave two maps that differ in what they can observe (Figure 2B). As in Figure 1, each result records phenotype applicability, eQTL and LD coverage, method eligibility, evidence state, unresolved alternative, and next discriminating experiment. Both methods report PP.H4, the posterior probability that the GWAS and eQTL associations share one variant. Single-variant ABF and multi-signal SuSiE colocalization have different eligibility requirements [ref: coloc ABF method; coloc-SuSiE method]. SuSiE needs converged eQTL and GWAS fits and usable LD, so a missing multi-signal result marks limited observability, not a negative result.

The genetic union contains 996 genes: 436 primary multi-signal genes and 560 single-signal-only genes. Seven genes remain SuSiE-untestable, three of them in the single-signal-only set, and they are not SuSiE negatives. Because the two methods can test different genes, we report each count against the genes that method could test, and we report single-signal-only genes separately. We ran colocalization twice. The second run repeated only the positives from the first run and added the shared-posterior check, which requires both signals in a pair to keep at least half of their posterior probability on variants that the GWAS and eQTL fits share. Negative results from the first run were carried forward unchanged. The count of finite results therefore includes these carried negatives, and we report it separately from the subset that passed the shared-posterior check. The GWAS fit is built on the variants of the eQTL fit, so only the eQTL signal can fail this check. As a sensitivity analysis, we applied the check to the 12,087 carried-forward negative gene-by-GWAS tests in the tier-1/2 studies. In 343 of them (2.8%), every signal pair failed, so the test became untestable; in 1,744, only some signal pairs failed and the call stayed the same; the other 10,000 passed (Methods). A carried negative that fails the check becomes untestable, not positive, so the colocalized genes do not change. Of the 436 primary SuSiE genes, 424 are protein-coding and 12 are lncRNA targets. Gene-level eQTL data do not identify transcript isoforms or give a biotype-specific enrichment denominator.

The COLOC gene counts are not derived from the 360 fine-mapped loci. Of the 436 multi-signal genes, 100 have a colocalized lead variant within 250 kb of a fine-mapped window from the same GWAS (89 within the window itself). Of the same 436 genes, 94 are supported only by the Million Veteran Program (MVP) European ALT, AST, and NAFLD GWAS [ref: MVP liver-trait GWAS], which had no standalone fine-mapping, and these three GWAS supply the strongest colocalization for 140 of the 436 genes. SuSiE was refit to the GWAS inside each gene-by-GWAS colocalization test, in the same way for every stratum, whether or not the stratum was fine-mapped on its own (Methods). To describe where the lead colocalized variants fall, we assigned one lead COLOC variant to each of the 996 genes (933 distinct lead variants) and classified it by overlap with transcript annotations (Figure 2C). In this gene-level summary, the lead variant lay in coding sequence or a splice site for 35 genes, in a UTR for 70, in a promoter for 104, in an intron for 524, and in intergenic sequence for 263. Of the 35 coding genes, 14 have the lead variant in their own coding sequence or splice site and 21 only in another gene's. For example, the lead variant of SAMM50 is the PNPLA3 variant, and that of MAU2 is the TM6SF2 variant.

We describe the complete result as a map of inherited variation relevant to MASLD and liver traits, and we reserve direct MASLD language for the tiered disease subset. We restrict regulatory language to supported GWAS-eQTL relationships; protein-altering and unresolved noncoding signals keep their own labels. The eQTL side reuses an existing panel of European-ancestry donors (Broadaway et al., 2024). This analysis therefore adds MASLD and liver-trait tiering, integration of multi-ancestry GWAS with a European liver-eQTL panel, fine-mapped DNA context, and comparison with diseased human liver, not a new molecular resource. A colocalized gene is a genetically anchored candidate; colocalization alone does not show that the gene mediates the trait association.

Ancestry labels describe the GWAS populations. They do not mean that molecular regulation was ancestry-matched in every analysis. The liver-eQTL panel is European, so a non-European GWAS paired with it tests cross-population sharing, not ancestry-matched liver regulation. We report LD and eQTL provenance for these pairs and do not treat them as ancestry replications (Figures 2E and 2F). Of the 436 multi-signal genes, 340 were supported only in European studies, 64 in European and at least one non-European study, and 32 only in non-European studies (Figure 2E). Of the 340 European-only genes, 213 were not tested in any non-European study, so their European-only status is untested rather than unsupported. Thirteen genes had PP.H4 > 0.5, by SuSiE or ABF, in four of the five GWAS ancestry groups, and no gene was supported in all five (Figure 2F). These partitions do not establish biological differences between ancestry groups.

Two locus views show what the Resource records for single genes (Figures 2G-2I). At RORA, the MVP European NAFLD GWAS colocalized with the liver eQTL (SuSiE PP.H4 0.983), although no variant in the window reached genome-wide significance (lead *p* = 7.6 x 10^-8) and this GWAS has no fine-mapping record (Figure 2G). At FABP1, the UK Biobank ALT GWAS colocalized with the liver eQTL (SuSiE PP.H4 0.998), but FABP1 was not differentially expressed in bulk liver (log2 fold change −0.04, *p*_adj = 0.51) (Figure 2H).

We next asked which trait class supplies each gene's strongest colocalization evidence. For 423 of the 436 genes it came from a liver-enzyme trait (ALT 137, AST 158, GGT 128), and for 13 from a direct disease or liver-fat trait (NAFLD 8, PDFF 4, NASH 1). Each gene is counted once, under the trait class of its strongest colocalization, so this split does not show how many genes each trait class supports. The split is similar in European studies alone: of the 404 genes with SuSiE PP.H4 > 0.5 in a European GWAS, 393 take their strongest European colocalization from a liver-enzyme trait. One gene, MAU2, has PP.H4 of 1.0 in the MVP European ALT, AST and NAFLD GWAS. Exact ties go to the GWAS whose study identifier sorts first alphabetically, so MAU2 is counted under the enzyme class (Methods). Counting any direct-trait support, 22 genes have a direct-trait SuSiE PP.H4 above 0.5: 12 direct only, 11 of them SuSiE-tested in an enzyme GWAS, and 10 with both classes. MVP European NAFLD is the only direct-trait support for 9 of the 22 genes and gives the strongest direct-trait PP.H4 for 11 of the 22. Among the 362 genes SuSiE-tested in both trait classes, 56 are enzyme-supported and 21 direct-supported (Figure 1C). Multi-signal colocalization tested far more enzyme than direct-trait gene-by-GWAS pairs (12,121 against 771), of which 739 (6.1%) and 28 (3.6%) colocalized. Most pairs in these denominators are first-run negatives carried forward. When the shared-posterior check was also applied to them, the denominators fell to 11,797 and 752 pairs, and the rates rose to 6.3% and 3.7%. In the first run, before any check, the rates were 787 of 12,132 (6.5%) and 29 of 771 (3.8%). These per-pair rates are descriptive. Pairs within a locus are not independent, and we made no test. The second run repeated only first-run positives (816 gene-by-GWAS pairs). The 171 direct and 3,844 enzyme SuSiE signal pairs (one GWAS signal with one eQTL signal) eligible in that rerun therefore do not measure per-signal yield, and 124 of the 171 direct pairs come from MVP European NAFLD, which has no standalone fine-mapping. Most colocalized genes thus take their strongest evidence from liver-enzyme traits, a pattern that largely follows how many gene-trait pairs were tested in each trait class.

## The Resource assigns each gene a genetic, disease-state or two-axis evidence class

Having defined the colocalized gene set, we compared it with the disease-state transcriptome from the bulk fit over 774 genetic-identity clusters, which are not verified donors (Figure 1B). Among the 3,769 stable-Ensembl genes evaluated by multi-signal colocalization, 426 were SuSiE-colocalized and 220 were canonical disease-state genes. The two sets shared 26 genes against 21.2 expected within expression deciles (Mantel-Haenszel OR 1.29, 95% CI 0.84 to 2.01; exact *p* = 0.28, one prespecified test). Expression level was not fully controlled, so we state no direction. The count is a description, not evidence that the two layers are biologically separate or convergent. A further 10,928 jointly tested genes, including 935 canonical disease-state genes, have no multi-signal COLOC result. Another 7 are SuSiE-untestable because, in one tier-1/2 study, no signal pair passed the shared-posterior check. Neither group is counted as negative. Another 699 canonical disease-state genes, 503 of them lncRNAs, have no tier-1/2 colocalization record and lie outside the 14,704 genes with both a bulk test and a colocalization record. Together with the 935 disease-state genes above that have no multi-signal result, this leaves 220 of the 1,854 calls in the comparison. The raw categorical universe contains 14,720 genes and the annotation-adjudicated Catalog universe 14,682; their denominators are not interchangeable.

Across the 3,769 genes, PP.H4 and the absolute disease-state log2 fold change had Spearman rho = 0.001 (nominal *p* = 0.95). Adding the seven SuSiE-untestable genes gives the 3,776 genes drawn in Figure 3G, with rho = 0.0014 (nominal *p* = 0.93). Because PP.H4 is unsigned, we did not test it against the signed fold change. Separating inherited genetic evidence from established tissue state has two practical consequences. First, differential expression cannot serve as a required validation filter for genetically anchored regulatory candidates. Second, absence of genetic support does not make a disease-state program unimportant, because common-variant colocalization has limited power and tests inherited regulation rather than tissue activity. We therefore assign each gene to an evidence class rather than treating either map as a noisy approximation of the other. Genetically anchored genes have SuSiE PP.H4 > 0.5 and no disease-versus-control RNA call. Established-state associated genes have an RNA call and no SuSiE PP.H4 > 0.5. Genes supported on both axes meet both thresholds; the direction of the genetic effect is not compared with the RNA direction. Unresolved genes are testable in both domains and meet neither threshold, and untested genes are absent from, or untestable in, at least one domain (Methods).

We next checked the colocalization state of the recurring disease-remodeling examples. None of GNMT, MAT1A, CYP2C19, IGFBP7, and BICC1 has a primary SuSiE PP.H4 above 0.5. Only GNMT has a single-signal ABF PP.H4 above 0.5, in one direct-NAFLD stratum (0.706, European NAFLD GWAS; Ghodsian et al., 2021). Its shared-variant anchor is at GRCh37 6:42931261 (rs2296804, SNP.PP.H4 0.259); SNP.PP.H4 is the posterior that a given variant is the shared one. The NAFLD association at this locus is weak: the anchor has GWAS p = 2.6 × 10⁻⁴, and the smallest p within 1 Mb is 1.4 × 10⁻⁴. The colocalization therefore rests mainly on the strong eQTL (p = 2.9 × 10⁻⁶⁶) and on the prior probability that one variant affects both traits (p12 = 5 × 10⁻⁶). In the earlier run of this pair (PP.H4 0.708 at that prior), PP.H4 was 0.33 at p12 = 1 × 10⁻⁶ and 0.83 at 1 × 10⁻⁵. We therefore treat GNMT's genetic support as sub-threshold sensitivity evidence. Multi-signal SuSiE colocalization was attempted for GNMT in that stratum but returned no credible-set result, and its best SuSiE PP.H4 in any primary study is 0.128 (UK Biobank ALT). The strongest stored ABF anchors for MAT1A, CYP2C19, IGFBP7, and BICC1 are at 10:81143387, 10:96292988, 4:57949517, and 10:60583832, respectively, but their gene-trait PP.H4 values remain below 0.5. These coordinates are COLOC shared-variant anchors, not causal variants or GWAS credible-set PIP.

We treat GNMT as a provisional worked locus. At the nonpalindromic variant rs2296805, the G allele lowers liver GNMT expression and aligns with higher direct-NAFLD risk. rs2296805 is a proxy for the rs2296804 anchor (UK Biobank European r2 = 0.998). The anchor is a C/G variant at a frequency near 0.5, so its allele labels do not resolve strand on their own. The nonpalindromic rs9471976 also aligns lower expression with higher risk and has r2 = 0.818 with rs2296805. rs11752813 (r2 = 0.580) is palindromic, and its reported sign is not independently harmonized. Ji et al. (2012) associated rs9471976 and rs11752813 with GNMT protein in 268 liver biopsies and reported allele-dependent reporter activity, which motivates resolving this locus. Unsigned LD and colocalization do not identify a causal variant or show that GNMT mediates the association.

Expression-based colocalization may miss some protein-altering and protein-level loci. Protein-altering variants can act through protein function without changing steady-state RNA, so they need not appear as expression QTLs; others change RNA abundance through nonsense-mediated decay or splicing (Rivas et al., 2015). SuSiE credible sets place a missense variant at three such genes: MTARC1 (rs2642438; normalized posterior inclusion probability, PIP, 0.757 in a European PDFF GWAS), TM6SF2 (rs58542926; PIP 1.0, European PDFF), and PNPLA3 (rs738409; PIP 0.706, MVP Admixed American NAFLD). All three genes are in the unresolved Catalog class, and external protein-QTL evidence for MTARC1 is a source finding (Gobeil et al., 2026b). The Gene Catalog records such members for every gene. In total, 31 genes carry a fine-mapped protein-altering or canonical-splice credible-set member at normalized SuSiE PIP ≥ 0.5 (20 unresolved, 7 genetically anchored, 3 established-state associated, 1 untested, and 0 supported on both axes). This field misses non-canonical splice effects, does not change the eQTL-colocalization class, and does not show that the gene mediates the trait association.

## Noncoding variants carry most fine-mapped posterior probability at liver-trait loci

To measure DNA architecture directly, we used every reliable tier-1/2 SuSiE credible set that fine-mapping produced, not only the credible sets that can be rebuilt from the top SNP of a colocalized gene. Of 6,467 study-by-locus credible-set instances, 6,415 passed the prespecified PIP-sum criterion. Figure 2D uses the 4,107 of these whose lead variant has p < 5 × 10⁻⁸ in its own GWAS (164 direct-trait and 3,943 enzyme credible sets; Methods). Complete GRCh37 reference-oriented VEP annotation [ref: Ensembl VEP] assigned 100% of their normalized PIP to a consequence class. We grouped credible sets into physical regions by single linkage, joining sets whose lead positions lie within 1 Mb of each other, so one region can span more than 1 Mb, and weighted each region equally. Direct MASLD and liver-fat loci then allocated 29.42% of the probability to protein-altering variants, 0.23% to canonical splice variants, 3.92% to synonymous or UTR variants, and 66.43% to other noncoding variants. Liver-enzyme loci allocated 6.67%, 0.00%, 3.50%, and 89.83% (Figure 2D). The grouping limits the weight of repeatedly recovered studies and does not assert LD or statistical independence. When each credible set was instead weighted equally, protein-altering variants carried 17.36% of the posterior for direct traits and 3.43% for liver enzymes.

Fine-mapping coverage differs by stratum. Every lead window of the European direct-trait GWAS that entered fine-mapping has a credible set (47 of 47). The European enzyme arm consists of 82 credible sets from the UK Biobank ALT, AST, and GGT GWAS (Sinnott-Armstrong et al., 2021). In the fine-mapping table used for Figure 2D, these GWAS have credible sets at 6, 9, and 15 loci and none at PNPLA3 or TM6SF2. These loci came from an earlier lead set, so they match 8 of the 767 current lead windows by locus identifier, while 23 of 767 have a credible set within 500 kb by position, the better coverage measure. A separate UKBB fine-mapping run at loci selected by colocalization (PP.H4 ≥ 0.5) is not used, because loci selected on colocalization cannot serve as an unbiased enzyme arm. The MVP European ALT, AST, and NAFLD GWAS had no standalone fine-mapping. In the Admixed American (AMR) GWAS, all 4 direct and all 35 enzyme lead windows at p < 5 × 10⁻⁸ have a credible set.

Taking protein-altering and canonical-splice variants together, direct and enzyme loci pooled over ancestries carry 29.65% and 6.67% of their posterior probability, respectively (difference 0.2298, 95% physical-cluster bootstrap interval 0.0808 to 0.3958; 120 physical clusters: 16 direct, 115 enzyme, and 11 shared). For this interval, we resampled whole clusters 10,000 times, because nearby credible sets are not independent. This pooled value is not a trait-class contrast. Of the direct credible sets, 106 of 164 are European, and 15 of the 16 direct clusters contain a European direct set, whereas 3,861 of 3,943 enzyme credible sets are non-European. Trait class is therefore confounded with GWAS ancestry, LD reference, and fine-mapping coverage.

To separate trait class from ancestry, we stratified the comparison by ancestry (Figure 2D, ancestry panel; 10,000 shared draws over 44 pooled clusters). European studies gave 29.88% against 6.92% (difference 0.2296, 95% interval 0.0563 to 0.4211), and AMR studies gave 8.11% against 6.91% (0.0120, −0.1246 to 0.1620). The prespecified fixed-weight European-plus-AMR estimate (weights 16/20 and 4/20, the direct-cluster counts) was 0.1861 (0.0503 to 0.3337). All non-European studies pooled gave 8.71% against 6.48% (0.0223, −0.0831 to 0.1686), a descriptive value with no calibration control. None of these estimates receives a trait-class call. The European estimate failed two checks: coverage (enzyme credible sets at 8 of 767 lead windows by locus identifier and 23 by position, direct arm at 47 of 47) and power (minimum detectable difference 0.303 against the pooled 0.230). It passed its random-split calibration control. In this control, we split East Asian enzyme clusters (East Asian and African clusters for the European-plus-AMR estimate) at random into two pseudo-classes with the observed cluster counts, 200 times. Both pseudo-classes come from one trait class, so a calibrated 95% interval should cover zero in at least 180 splits, and the 98.333% interval (Bonferroni over the three estimates) in at least 190. The European 95% and 98.333% intervals covered zero in 183 and 191 splits. AMR coverage passed (4 of 4 direct and 35 of 35 enzyme lead windows), but the AMR and European-plus-AMR estimates failed the control (122 and 172 splits at 95%; 135 and 188 at 98.333%), so their intervals are too narrow. Both also narrowly failed the power check (minimum detectable differences 0.234 and 0.232), and in 156 of the 10,000 draws the AMR direct arm, 4 clusters, was empty, more than the prespecified limit of 100. The European-plus-AMR estimate also carries the European coverage failure.

Within the direct class, the three European PDFF GWAS (37 credible sets, 12 clusters) carry 40.14% against 6.67% for all enzyme traits (difference 0.3347, 95% interval 0.1492 to 0.5280). This comparison sets European PDFF studies against an enzyme arm that is mostly non-European, so it carries the same ancestry confounding as the pooled value. NAFLD plus NASH (127 sets, 8 clusters) carry 15.92% (0.0925, −0.0596 to 0.2844) (Figure S2S). The pooled direct-minus-enzyme difference is also concentrated. Five of the 16 direct clusters carry 73.9% of the summed direct cluster fractions, and the largest, one PDFF credible set at chr11:823,586, carries 21.1%. The 50 robustness comparisons (study and direct-cluster omissions, five grouping windows, and one LD-block comparison) gave ratios from 3.44 to 5.18 around the pooled reference ratio of 4.45, a descriptive value. These comparisons include no ancestry or trait stratification and are correlated sensitivities, not replications.

The two trait classes in the 29.65% against 6.67% comparison also differ in GWAS size and effect size. Direct-trait credible sets come from smaller GWAS than enzyme credible sets (median effective n 21,893 against 160,000 over the 4,134 credible sets at genome-wide leads) and carry larger standardized lead-variant effects (median 0.0423 against 0.0190). Supplementary Note 4 reports a sensitivity analysis matched on standardized lead-variant effect, over the same 4,134 credible sets, including those that failed the PIP-sum criterion. No permutation *p* value is reported. Comparing the trait classes within one ancestry requires SuSiE fine-mapping of all 767 UK Biobank ALT, AST, and GGT lead windows and of the MVP European ALT, AST, and NAFLD GWAS. This comparison does not test whether the pooled coding difference contributes to the predominance of liver-enzyme evidence among colocalized genes, or to which genes appear in both the colocalized and disease-state sets.

Within the noncoding posterior probability, we treated promoter, liver ABC-enhancer (Nasser et al., 2021), and liver-lineage open-chromatin annotations as overlapping contexts, not as probabilities that must sum to one. After re-annotation by genomic position (Supplementary Note 6), no noncoding posterior probability was left without a context call. Summed over all credible sets of each class at genome-wide leads, 14.93% of direct-trait and 14.01% of liver-enzyme noncoding posterior probability lies within 2 kb of a TSS, 10.73% and 7.84% in liver-lineage open chromatin, 0.30% and 3.85% in a liver ABC enhancer, and 75.39% and 78.28% in sequence with none of these contexts. These pooled values mix ancestries. AMR is the one ancestry with comparable fine-mapping coverage in both classes. Within AMR, no context difference received a call (99% intervals, Bonferroni over five contexts). Every random-split calibration control failed, and in 167 of the 10,000 draws the AMR direct arm, 4 clusters, was empty, more than the prespecified limit of 100. For the share with no deposited context, direct minus enzyme was −0.209 (99% interval −0.599 to 0.166). Thus, most noncoding fine-mapped probability lies outside the deposited promoter, open-chromatin, and ABC annotations, and the DNA result does not assign a regulatory mechanism to most noncoding signals.

## A bulk-liver accessibility QTL shares the GWAS signal at 191 of 581 colocalized lead signals

We next asked whether some of these signals share a variant with an accessible chromatin region in bulk liver. We took 581 European tier-1/2 GWAS lead signals that colocalize with a liver eQTL. For each, we tested whether a chromatin accessibility QTL measured in bulk liver from 138 donors (Currin et al., 2025) shares the same GWAS signal (PP.H4 > 0.8 and LD r² ≥ 0.5 between the two lead variants). Of these signals, 191 share a signal with a bulk-liver accessible region, and Currin et al. (2025) already reported at least 45 of them for the same trait and accessible region. Another 315 map to a different variant, 60 had no testable accessible region, 9 colocalized without confirmed lead LD, and 6 were indeterminate. Of the 436 primary SuSiE genes, 143 have a colocalized accessible region. In 108 of them, the same accessible region also colocalizes with the gene's eQTL. This comparison uses no lead-variant LD rule. When the accessibility-QTL and eQTL lead variants must also have r² ≥ 0.8, 100 of the 108 remain (107 at r² ≥ 0.5). The other 35 genes (43 under the r² ≥ 0.8 rule) are reached only through the GWAS-eQTL pair. Two pairwise colocalizations with one GWAS signal do not show that the accessibility QTL and the eQTL share a variant, so we do not link these genes to the region. The eQTL and accessibility-QTL donor sets may overlap, so the three-way support is not independent replication.

To estimate how often a shared accessible region occurs, we excluded chromosomes 1 and 19, which a pilot analysis had examined. Of the remaining 238 independent liver-trait loci, 80 (0.336, Wilson 95% CI 0.279 to 0.398) carry a shared accessible region. This rate is conditional on prior liver-eQTL colocalization and is not the share of liver GWAS loci with an accessibility QTL. We then compared loci selected the same way that carry signals from one trait arm only (172 liver-trait loci and 78 platelet-count loci). At equal GWAS and accessibility-QTL strength, liver-trait signals shared a liver accessibility QTL more often than platelet-count signals (adjusted odds ratio 3.35, 95% CI 1.62 to 7.40; *p* = 0.0016, one prespecified test). The unadjusted counts, 70 of 172 against 11 of 78, are descriptive. This agrees with the trait-level pattern reported by Currin et al. (2025), and we do not present it as new.

Taken together, these analyses define the genetic arm of the Resource as a bounded map of trait-associated inherited variation, fine-mapped molecular context, and colocalized liver-trait regulatory candidates. This map does not substitute for the established disease-state transcriptome, which we describe next.
